## CARE-Bench: measure action timing and information sufficiency

**Question and design.** This benchmark asks what action a patient-facing system communicates at each patient-disclosure prefix. It is not an institutional queue-routing study. Section 3.1 defines four actions: targeted clarification, self-care/monitoring, nonurgent care, and urgent care. Urgent care explicitly includes **same-day, emergency, or otherwise delay-unsafe care**, so that label cannot directly serve as this project's separate same-day-review and emergency-guidance oracle.

Section 3.3 describes reconstruction from existing medical dialogue/consultation/follow-up-question sources. GPT-5.5 serves as constructor, reviewer, and reviser. A public-health PhD candidate screened flagged cases; a medical trainee reviewed cases needing clinical judgment; both discussed unresolved cases. This is a materially narrower review process than a large independent clinician panel, and use of the same model family in construction and judging creates a potential structural bias to examine. The retained cases contain one to three prefixes. They are accumulated patient text, not a live rollout containing prior assistant responses.

Table 1 reports 500 cases and 1,059 prefixes overall. Evaluation uses 123 held-out cases/269 prefixes, including 74 insufficient-information and 51 urgent-care prefixes. Eleven models each generated one response per prefix under unprompted and minimally prompted protocols, with a 300-token ceiling or nearest supported equivalent. Section 3.5 uses a fixed GPT-5.5 mapper, blinded to the gold label, future turns, and source metadata, to classify open-ended responses. It reports 1,000 case-clustered bootstrap replicates because multiple prefixes share a case.

**Results and tradeoffs.** Table 2 reports unprompted macro-F1 31.2–50.4 and prompted macro-F1 46.9–63.4; prompting improved ten of eleven models. These are benchmark results, not estimates for our clinic workflow. Table 3 shows insufficient-information recall averaged across models rising from 19.0% to only 33.5%; premature nonurgent recommendations were common. Prompted urgent-care recall averaged 75.8%. Aggregate improvement did not imply improvement on every failure type: the paper describes models reducing missed care while increasing unnecessary care or over-referral, and overlapping intervals do not support a stable fine-grained leaderboard.

**Measurement limitations.** The Limitations section says reconstruction can produce cleaner language and more cooperative information disclosure than real users. Institution-specific availability and routing are compressed into four labels. Appendix J audited 120 balanced, challenge-enriched outputs with two independent reviewers: exact-action mapper agreement was 95.0% with Reviewer 1 but 85.0% with Reviewer 2, while inter-reviewer agreement was 90.0%. Those are audit-sample statistics, not prevalence-weighted reliability estimates. The token-budget screen flagged 539/5,918 outputs (9.1%) as potentially affected by truncation; it did not measure longer-budget performance. No generation or mapping failures occurred in the reported held-out runs, so this study does not supply evidence for our infrastructure fallbacks.

**Implications for this prototype.** Evaluate intermediate judgments and final route, including temporal variants and insufficient-information cases. Keep sufficiency separate from urgency, retain emergency versus same-day distinctions, and count errors at each threshold. Direct-label classification is a valid model interface—the companion repository explicitly supports it—and can avoid an extra generative-response mapper. It still requires testing against an appropriate locally defined oracle. Neither this benchmark nor its model ranking chooses Jev, a decomposed design, or a general-purpose LLM for us. CARE-Bench explicitly disclaims use to automate routing, certify deployment, or compare safety across care systems.

## Randomized public-use study: model accuracy does not establish workflow performance

**Design and methods.** The study recruited 1,298 English-speaking UK adults through Prolific and collected 2,400 interactions, 600 per arm. Participants were randomized to GPT-4o, Llama 3, Command R+, or their usual information sources. The model interfaces concealed model identity, while control participants necessarily knew they did not receive an LLM. Demographic stratification targeted the UK population. Participants completed up to two of ten physician-authored scenarios and selected a disposition on a five-point UK-specific care scale, then named conditions informing their choice. Three doctors agreed the target disposition; four other doctors supplied differential diagnoses for the condition endpoint.

Data were collected 21 August–14 October 2024. Human-study design and analysis were preregistered; direct-model experiments were added later to interpret the findings. Methods disclose replacement of **98 participants following API timeouts**, replacement of 13 assigned across multiple arms through a platform error, and exclusion of 493 starters who did not finish. These issues changed the stopping rule from exactly 300 participants per arm with two interactions to 600 interactions per arm with a maximum of two per participant. The paper describes no association between treatment and the 101 dropouts exposed to treatment. This is transparent reporting, but performance among completed/replaced interactions is not an end-to-end reliability estimate that includes every outage.

**Results.** In the direct-model baseline, disposition accuracy was 64.7% for GPT-4o, 48.8% for Llama 3, and 55.5% for Command R+; condition identification was substantially higher, illustrating why diagnostic-knowledge and action endpoints must be separated. The participant study found **no statistically significant disposition-accuracy difference** between any model arm and control (reported p-values 0.683, 0.560, and 0.861). The overall correct disposition rate was 43.0% ± 2.0% as reported. Control participants were better at identifying relevant conditions; that diagnosis-related endpoint is not a feature or objective of our prototype.

A qualitative sample of 30 interactions found incomplete initial information in 16, with later added symptoms in seven of those. Models also supplied misleading or inconsistent responses, and participants did not reliably select accurate suggestions. The authors observed jurisdictional errors in emergency contact guidance. The simulated-patient comparison used 300 GPT-4o-simulated conversations and produced different, less variable behavior: 26/30 scenario-by-arm combinations had either 0% or 100% disposition accuracy across ten trials. Synthetic model interlocutors did not reproduce human interaction variability.

**Limits and application.** These were ten invented scenarios, paid participants, English-language UK users, 2024 models, and a particular interface—not this administrative prototype or Jev. Vignettes avoid real symptom stress; repeated scenario observations do not provide thousands of independent clinical situations. The model-alone comparisons were not preregistered. This evidence does not establish that all current models fail, that structured routing cannot help, or that Jev is superior. It does establish a concrete reason to test communication and human actions separately from model outputs.

For this prototype, test recognition of immediate emergency guidance, completion without hiding material facts, interpretation of received versus reviewed versus booked, and staff response to uncertainty. Configure jurisdiction explicitly; do not let a model invent emergency numbers. Count failed saves, timeouts, and unreviewed queue items in reliability results, even if classification metrics also report successful calls separately. A synthetic application test suite is not a substitute for later human usability evaluation. No human participant evaluation of this prototype has been performed.

## DECIDE-AI: use reporting discipline without claiming clinical evaluation

**Method and output.** The guideline used a two-round modified Delphi process, a 16-person consensus group with an 80% inclusion threshold, and qualitative review by 16 independent experts. There were 123 first-round and 138 second-round respondents, with 151 distinct experts across 18 countries and 20 stakeholder categories. The final checklist has 17 AI-specific items, comprising 28 subitems, plus ten generic items. This is consensus-based reporting guidance, not an intervention efficacy trial.

**Relevant checklist items.** Table 2 directly supports reporting intended users/settings (2 and 5), versioned systems and missing inputs (4), safety-error definitions and rates (6, 13, 16), human factors (7 and 14), modifications during a study (11), and reasons for human disagreement or changed decisions (12). Item 13 distinguishes recommendation errors, supporting software/hardware malfunctions, and user errors. This supports recording failed persistence and queue handoff failures alongside model mistakes. The guideline treats users as a study population rather than assuming that a clinician's presence guarantees correction.

**Scope and limitations.** Box 2 defines clinical/live evaluation as use affecting actual patients; in-silico and scenario simulations are distinct. Our prototype and invented fixtures therefore cannot be labeled a DECIDE-AI live clinical evaluation. The discussion explicitly says the checklist is a reporting standard, not a method-quality tool or regulatory approval substitute. Selection/anchoring biases remain possible, participants skewed toward Europe/UK, clinicians and engineers were overrepresented, and few early clinical studies existed when the candidate items were assembled. Interpretability and trust were not included as stand-alone items because accepted measures were lacking; the authors explicitly do not regard them as unimportant. Do not claim the guideline mandates our explanation format. Our factual input/rule trace is a task-specific design choice whose fidelity and usability still need evaluation.

## FUTURE-AI: lifecycle accountability, calibrated uncertainty, and explainability limits

**Method and output.** FUTURE-AI used a 24-month, eight-round modified Delphi process, reaching 117 experts in 50 countries. Initial working groups came from five European medical-imaging projects; the scope broadened to healthcare in general. The final output contains 30 recommendations across fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, explainability, plus a general category. Final disagreement was below 5%, chiefly about whether an item should be recommended or highly recommended for research versus deployable systems. Agreement is not measured clinical benefit.

**Specific recommendations used.** Universality 1 and 4 require specifying setting and evaluating local validity. Traceability 1–6 cover risk management, documentation, input/output quality control, monitoring, logging, and governance. Traceability 3 discusses uncertainty estimates **and calibration**, which supports avoiding claims that raw vendor confidence is clinical safety probability. Traceability 5 calls for logging inputs/predictions/clinical decisions/issues in a privacy-preserving manner. Usability 2 calls for use-case-specific oversight and correction. Explainability 1–2 require defining user needs, testing explanation correctness, and assessing whether explanations inappropriately increase confidence. General 4 calls for separated test data, suitable metrics, and comparison with reference tools or standard practice.

**Limits.** The discussion deliberately avoids prescribing one implementation technique; applicability depends on the clinical use case. It highlights uncertain standards, liability/governance questions, costs for resource-limited settings, and the need for continued revision. The patient/public involvement statement says direct involvement was not feasible because dedicated funding was absent. Although the consortium includes diverse expertise, that must not be misrepresented as direct patient co-design. This broad consensus is useful for organizing evidence and responsibility; it cannot validate our administrative rules or model choice.

## Decisions this evidence supports

1. Retain all four comparison arms: structured form plus human review; direct Jev classification with defer; decomposed Jev assessments plus rules; equivalent structured-output LLM assessments. Keep immediate structured emergency guidance identical in all arms.
2. Measure both raw model decisions and safeguarded actions. Clinical urgency, information sufficiency, service availability, and persistence success are separate variables.
3. Report emergency misses, incorrect routine suggestions, unnecessary escalation, reviewer workload, timing, and infrastructure failure. Use numerator/denominator and mark empty denominators as not estimable. Do not summarize with overall accuracy alone.
4. Test the patient/staff communication and queue workflow, including wrong jurisdiction, pending assessment, unowned/overdue items, and failed saves. Immediate emergency direction must survive model and server failures.
5. Preserve original input, normalized assessments, actual executed rule IDs, versions, and staff overrides. A plausible model rationale is not proof that a rule executed or that a decision is medically correct.
6. Keep synthetic tests and clinical claims separate. Team policy, jurisdiction, supported populations, staffing, escalation contacts, and acceptable error thresholds are still prerequisites for any real deployment; these papers do not supply them.

No route threshold, deadline, or emergency checklist in the demonstration becomes clinically approved through these citations. No new patient records were drawn from the papers. For Jev-specific research and current model experiments, see the separately maintained Jev report and actual run artifacts rather than inferring their outcomes from this workflow review.
